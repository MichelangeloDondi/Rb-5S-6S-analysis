## What the paper covers, in outline

The abstract (p.791) states its own scope directly: "This review describes atomic transitions
where two or more photons are simultaneously absorbed by an atom or a molecule (without any
intermediate resonant level)." Six sections, per the Contents page (p.792): Section 2 is the principle
(Doppler cancellation, lineshape, transition probability, light shifts). Section 3 is the experimental
demonstration (dye-laser technique, a worked sodium two-photon example, a two-colour variant, a
three-photon sodium experiment). Section 4 is a review of what two-photon spectroscopy had by 1977 been used
to measure (fine/hyperfine structure, Zeeman/Stark splitting, isotopic shifts, collisional shifts
and broadening, absolute wavelength/frequency measurements including hydrogen 1S-2S, molecular
spectroscopy, transient effects). Section 5 is the tensorial formalism for computing two-photon line
intensities and selection rules, worked through a neon example. Section 6 is a comparison with competing
Doppler-free methods (classical double-resonance/level-crossing, atomic beams, saturated
absorption). The introduction (p.793) explicitly excludes stepwise excitation (a real intermediate
state, i.e. two sequential one-photon transitions) from the review's scope, restricting it to
transitions with no real intermediate level: true multiphoton absorption in Göppert-Mayer's
(1931) sense.

This review is not a distant survey of the technique: its own text says the cw dye laser it
describes in Sec 3.1 is "used in our laboratory in Paris" (p.803), Sec 3 attributes the first
standing-wave two-photon demonstration to "Biraben *et al* (1974a)... in Paris" (p.803, the same
city as the review's own byline affiliation), and several of its central worked figures are
explicitly reproduced from Biraben's and Bjorkholm/Liao's own papers: Fig 9 "(Biraben *et al*
1974b)" (p.809), Fig 10 "(from Bjorkholm and Liao 1974)" (p.810), Fig 21 "(from Biraben *et al*
1977)" (p.820), Fig 22 "(from Biraben *et al* 1975b)" (p.822), with Fig 8's own 3S-5S recording
also credited to the same group in the surrounding text (Biraben *et al* 1974d, Biraben 1977,
p.808) even though its caption itself carries no "(from...)" tag. Taken together this supports
reading Grynberg and Cagnac as writing from inside the same Paris programme that produced most of
the two-photon sodium results the review presents in detail, instead of as outside surveyors of
it, though the paper nowhere states this relationship in so many words and no institutional or
biographical claim beyond what these citations and the shared affiliation line show is made here.
It is the direct lineage source for the already-held `biraben1974.md`, `biraben1979.md`,
`biraben2019.md`, `bjorkholm1976.md`, `borde1976.md`, `borde1976b.md`, `camparo1992.md` and
`camparo1992b.md` (all confirmed present in `docs/lit/` beforehand), several of
which this review cites directly by the same 1974-1976 references.

## The Doppler-free lineshape (Sec 2.1-2.3, pp.795-798)

Section 2.1 derives the standing-wave cancellation from energy conservation for an atom of
velocity **V** absorbing one photon from each of two counter-propagating beams of the same
angular frequency ω:

$$E_\text{e}-E_\text{g}=\hbar(\omega+\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{V})+\hbar(\omega-\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{V})=2\hbar\omega \qquad (2.1)$$

with the text's own reading: "The terms depending on the velocity of the atom disappear,
indicating that, at resonance, *all the atoms, irrespective of their velocities, can absorb two
photons*. Theoretically, the width of this resonance is of the same order of magnitude as the
natural width." (p.795, checked against the rendered page.) Section 2.2 generalises this to *n*
photons via the momentum-conservation condition $\sum_{i=1}^n\boldsymbol{k}_i=0$ (Eq 2.2, p.795)
paired with the energy-conservation condition $E_\text{e}-E_\text{g}=\sum_{i=1}^n\hbar ck_i$ (Eq
2.3, p.796).

Section 2.3 then derives *why* the resonance is not a pure Lorentzian: off resonance, only atoms
of one velocity group can satisfy the two-photon condition by absorbing both photons from the
*same* travelling wave (a residual, velocity-selected, Doppler-broadened process), while at exact
resonance every atom can absorb one photon from each of the two counter-propagating waves. The
lineshape is stated as the superposition of two curves (p.797, checked against the rendered page,
quoted as printed with the paper's own emphasis):

> "A Lorentzian curve of large intensity and narrow width (natural width) corresponding to the
> absorption of photons from the oppositely travelling waves. A Gaussian curve of small intensity
> and broad width (Doppler width) corresponding to the absorption of photons from the same
> travelling wave."

For identical intensity and polarization counter-propagating beams the paper derives the *area*
ratio between the two curves algebraically (summing intensities, i.e. incoherently, for the
Gaussian background versus summing *amplitudes* coherently for the two indistinguishable
resonant paths that make up the Lorentzian, since the process is "absorption of a photon of the
incident wave followed by the absorption of a photon of the reflected wave" interfering with the
reverse order): the Lorentzian's area is twice the Gaussian's, i.e. four times a single
travelling-wave two-photon signal against twice one for the Doppler background. And, in the
paper's own words but with its Γₑ and ΔνD (natural and Doppler width) symbols spelled out instead
of quoted, since this scan's text layer renders Greek letters as unrelated Latin ones here (Γₑ
becomes the two letters `re`, ΔνD the four letters `AVD`) instead of as the symbols themselves:
the intensity of the narrow curve is of the order of ΔνD/Γₑ larger than the intensity of the
Gaussian curve, so that in most cases the Gaussian Doppler curve appears as a very small
background (p.797, checked against the rendered page). This is the original Vasilenko et al
(1970) prediction. The paper's own attribution reads, in substance, that this lineshape was first
predicted by Vasilenko et al (1970), paraphrased instead of quoted because its own OCR text layer
renders `al` as the digit-letter pair `a1` at this spot (p.797). This is the same lineage
`delone1980.md`'s own use-in-this-record section already traces forward through Camparo and
Lambropoulos (1992).

The paper also notes (p.798) that for an S-S two-photon transition ($L=0\to L=0$) excited by
$\sigma^+/\sigma^-$ counter-propagating polarizations, the $\Delta m=0$ selection rule for the
cross-beam (Doppler-free) term survives while the same-beam $\Delta m=\pm2$ term is forbidden by
the same S-S selection rule, so the Doppler background vanishes identically and "the lineshape is
then just a Lorentzian curve" for that specific polarization choice, a qualitative point, not a
number this record uses.

## Transition probability, focusing, and the transit-time/light-shift trade-off (Sec 2.4-2.5, pp.798-802)

Second-order perturbation theory gives the two-photon excitation probability (Eq 2.4, p.798):

$$\mathscr{P}_{\text{ge}}^{(2)}(\delta\omega)=\Big|\sum_i\frac{\langle \text{e}|\mathscr{H}_1|i\rangle\langle i|\mathscr{H}_2|\text{g}\rangle+\langle \text{e}|\mathscr{H}_2|i\rangle\langle i|\mathscr{H}_1|\text{g}\rangle}{\Delta\omega_i}\Big|^2\frac{\Gamma_\text{e}}{4\delta\omega^2+\tfrac14\Gamma_\text{e}^2}$$

with a numerical worked example (p.800) giving $\mathscr{P}_{\text{ge}}^{(2)}(\text{res})=1\ \text{s}^{-1}$
for $P/S=1\ \text{W}\text{mm}^{-2}$ under stated typical hypotheses (Clebsch-Gordan product
$\sim1/10$, oscillator strengths $\sim1/10$, $\lambda_{gr}\sim\lambda_{re}\sim6000$ Å,
$\Gamma_\text{e}=10^8\ \text{s}^{-1}$). The paper then states plainly, and this is directly
relevant to this record's own waist/geometry chapter: "We notice the advantage of reducing the
cross section $S$ of the light beam, because the number of irradiated atoms decreases as $S$,
whereas the transition probability increases as" $1/S^2$ (this scan's text layer renders the
superscript as `l/SZ`, so the formula is given in the note's own LaTeX instead of quoted). "The
signal varies as $1/S$." (p.800, checked against the rendered page.)

But focusing has a floor, attributed to Hänsch *et al* (1974) and quoted at length because it is
the same trade-off this record's own waist chapter (`docs/methods/03_the_ac_stark_ramp.md`, its
section The waist as the second axis) works through for a different reason:

> "In fact, as was shown by Hänsch *et al* (1974) there is a limit to the desirability of
> focusing... The radius of the Gaussian beam is practically constant only over a length which is
> of the order of twice the Rayleigh range... For a given observation length $l$, it is useless to
> decrease $W_0$ below the value corresponding to $2Z_\text{R}\sim l$. By decreasing $W_0$ further
> one only obtains a small increase in signal, but some new problems may arise, such as saturation
> of the two-photon transition, light shift (§2.6) and modification of the lineshape due to the
> small transit time (Bordé 1976)." (p.800, checked against the rendered page.)

So as early as 1977, the same three effects this record's own model tracks as a focused beam is
tightened, namely saturation, the light shift, and a transit-time modification of the lineshape, are
named together as the reasons a two-photon experiment does not simply focus arbitrarily tightly.
`borde1976.md` is already held on this shelf for exactly the transit-time citation made here.
Section 2.5 (pp.801-802) extends the transition-probability estimate to $n$-photon processes via
a recursive one-intermediate-state comparison (Eq 2.6, p.801), used later in Sec 3.5's three-photon
sodium experiment.

## Light shifts (Sec 2.6, pp.802-803, revisited Sec 3.5, pp.812-814)

This is the section the task's own framing asked to be checked most carefully, and the review does
give a light-shift treatment of its own, but its purpose and shape differ from this record's.
The section opens (p.802, checked against the rendered page):

> "Owing to the elimination of Doppler broadening, the multiphotonic transition provides a
> powerful tool for spectroscopy. However, for this purpose, it is important to consider whether
> there might be any systematic factors which might shift the resonances. For instance, the light
> intensity which is required to induce these multiphotonic transitions might lead to large light
> shifts (or dynamical Stark effects). In fact, we show hereafter that these light shifts are
> generally small in the case of two-photon transitions, but they can be of some importance for
> higher multiphotonic processes (Cagnac *et al* 1973)."

The derivation uses a one-intermediate-state approximation (level $r$) and five stated
assumptions (identical counter-propagating beams, an unsaturated resonance, one dominant relay
level, the shift from virtual absorption through $r$ no larger than from virtual emission, and
comparable ground/excited matrix elements to $r$) to bound the ground- and excited-state shifts,

$$\delta\omega_\text{g}=2\frac{\langle \text{g}|\mathscr{H}|r\rangle\langle r|\mathscr{H}|\text{g}\rangle}{\Delta\omega_r}\ \ (2.8),\qquad
\delta\omega_\text{e}=2\frac{\langle \text{e}|\mathscr{H}|r\rangle\langle r|\mathscr{H}|\text{e}\rangle}{\Delta\omega_r}\ \ (2.9),$$

and combines them with the on-resonance transition probability (Eq 2.7) to give the working
condition $\delta\omega_\text{e}\delta\omega_\text{g}=\tfrac14\mathscr{P}_{\text{ge}}^{(2)}(\text{res})\Gamma_\text{e}\ll\Gamma_\text{e}^2$
(Eq 2.10, p.802), from which, under the fifth assumption, the light-shift-induced displacement of
the two-photon line ($\delta\omega_\text{e}-\delta\omega_\text{g}$) is argued much smaller than
the natural linewidth. The conclusion (p.803, checked against the rendered page):

> "We can conclude that, in most usual cases, the light shifts do not limit the use of two-photon
> spectroscopy in high-resolution experiments. Nevertheless, one has to be cautious. To attain the
> ultimate precision, it is important to verify that all the preceding assumptions are true in the
> particular case under consideration."

And immediately after, the paper names its own counter-example and its own limit case: "Liao and
Bjorkholm (1975) have observed in their experiment a light shift large compared with the
transition probability, but in their case assumption (i) was not verified because the two
oppositely travelling waves had different intensities and different frequencies" (p.803), and a
metastable-level excitation is flagged as a second case where the argument fails outright because
assumption (iv) breaks down. Critically, for three or more photons the paper states the argument
does not transfer at all: "Such a statement is not true in a higher multiphotonic process. This
can be understood in the following way: two-photon transitions and light shifts appear at the
same order of interaction with photons... a three-photon process, for instance, is a higher-order
process which is not directly related to the light shift." (p.803.) Section 3.5's own three-photon
sodium experiment (pp.811-814) demonstrates this directly: at a 10 GHz one-photon energy defect,
raising the laser power from 25 to 45 mW visibly shifts the three-photon resonance (Fig 14, p.814,
attributed to Biraben 1977), and the text attributes the broadening at higher intensity partly to
"spatial non-uniformities of the intensity within the region of observation" and partly to the
light shift varying "from one atomic velocity group to another because the Doppler broadening is
not very small compared to the energy gap" $\Delta\omega$ between the laser and the intermediate
level (p.813, the symbol itself is given in the note's own LaTeX, since this scan's text layer
renders it as unrelated Latin letters): an inhomogeneous light
shift across a spatially and thermally varying sample, which is a structurally similar concern to
(though not the same mechanism as) this record's own kernel-inhomogeneity chapter.

**What this section does not contain, stated plainly per the task's own instruction.** Nothing in
Sec 2.6 or Sec 3.5 treats the light shift as a *distribution* over the beam's transverse intensity
profile, or extracts information from the resulting lineshape asymmetry. The whole of Sec 2.6 is
a bound-and-avoid argument at fixed, idealised beam geometry ("the atom interacts with two
identical oppositely travelling waves") aimed at showing that conditions can usually be chosen
where the light shift is negligible against the natural width. It is not this record's
inverse-problem framing (`delone1980.md`'s Eq 5.3, already on this shelf, is the closer ancestor
of that idea, and is a later, different 1980 paper) nor this record's own derivation of the
triangular "ramp" distribution of shifts sampled by atoms across a focused Gaussian beam
(`docs/methods/03_the_ac_stark_ramp.md` §2.6). Grynberg and Cagnac's light shift is a systematic
correction to a line centre. This record's is a first-class, signal-bearing lineshape asymmetry.
The two share only the underlying physical fact (an intensity-dependent shift from the same beam
that drives the transition) and the historical observation, given here in 1977, that it is small
compared to Γₑ under ordinary two-photon conditions, which is also this record's own §2.6
finding for the *centroid* channel, even though the record then goes on to use the higher moments
of the same shift as its main signal instead of treating them only as a systematic to bound.

## Apparatus and sodium/three-photon results (Sec 3.1-3.5, pp.803-814)

Read closely for methodological and numerical precedent, not for anything this record borrows
directly. The cw dye-laser apparatus (Fig 6, p.804) uses two servo loops: an internal
Fabry-Pérot etalon for single-mode selection, and an external, thermally and pressure-stabilised
etalon locking the laser frequency, giving under 10 MHz drift over a few hours and about 1 MHz
jitter (p.804-5). The two-photon resonance is detected via fluorescence at a wavelength different
from the exciting one, specifically to reject scattered laser light with an interference filter or
monochromator (p.806), the same detection logic (a population monitor at a different wavelength
from the drive) this record's own PMT-based detection uses, though on a different transition and
apparatus. For the worked sodium 3S-5S example (Fig 8, p.808), the observed resonance width is
about 9 MHz on the laser-frequency scale (18 MHz on the atomic-energy scale, since two photons are
summed), with the paper's own attribution: "Most of it is due to the jitter of the laser because
the width due to the natural lifetime and the transit time through the light beam corresponds to
about 5 MHz (energy scale)." (p.807, checked against the rendered page): an explicit natural-plus-transit
combined width figure, on a different alkali S-S line, offered here only as period precedent for
the same three-way width budget (natural, laser jitter, transit) this record's own lineshape
chapter carries for Rb 5S-6S. Sections 3.3-3.4 also give the sodium 3S 5S hyperfine splitting
(155.1 ± 1 MHz, Biraben 1977, p.808) and describe the two-colour technique (Bjorkholm and Liao
1974, Fig 10, p.810) that trades a controlled residual Doppler width (about 60 MHz) for several
orders of magnitude more transition probability by tuning one photon near an intermediate
resonance. This is context for the already-held `bjorkholm1976.md`, not a number this record uses.

## Collisional lineshape (Sec 4.4.1, pp.819-821, and Sec 5.5, pp.836-837)

This is the most directly load-bearing connection to this record's own lineshape chapter. Section
4.4.1 opens: "The theory of the lineshape in the presence of collisions in the impact
approximation is a straightforward extension of the works of Anderson (1949) and Baranger (1958a,
b, c)... for the case of single-photon absorption and of Fiutak and Van Kranendonk (1962) for the
Raman effect case." (p.819, checked against the rendered page): the identical Baranger citation
`docs/methods/02_the_lineshape.md` §2.2 already uses for this record's own impact-approximation
justification of $\gamma_\text{coll}=\beta_\text{self}N$. The paper continues: "It follows that in
conditions which are usually satisfied" (§5.5, whose own section-symbol notation this scan's text
layer renders as a dollar sign followed by the three characters `5 S`) "the velocity-changing
collisions and the influence of the intermediate
state $r$ can be neglected: the lineshape thus appears (as in Raman spectra) to be the sum of
Lorentzian curves. Furthermore, it can be shown that in most cases there is only one single
Lorentzian curve" (again §5.5). (p.819.) Section 5.5 (pp.836-7) supplies the argument for why there
is only one Lorentzian in the two-photon (as opposed to one-photon Raman) case
specifically: the two-photon transition operator's two possible tensor ranks (scalar and
quadrupolar, derived in Sec 5.1) can in general carry two different collisional broadening
constants, but collapse to a single Lorentzian either when the operator is purely one rank for the
transition in question, or, more generally, even if the operator is a superposition of two
tensors: "when the interaction potential is much larger in the excited state than in the initial
state, all the optical multipoles have the same broadening (Omont 1973)." (p.837, checked against the rendered
page). This is a genuine extension beyond a bare citation of Baranger: it is the two-photon-specific
argument for exactly the single-Lorentzian, additive-width picture (natural ⊗ collisional, and
separately natural ⊗ laser, combining into one Lorentzian of summed width) this record's own
lineshape chapter uses without re-deriving. The experimental section (Figs 19-21, pp.819-821)
also shows, for two different sodium two-photon lines perturbed by the same noble gas, collisional
shifts of *opposite sign* (blue for 3S-5S, red for 3S-4D, both with neon), offered here only as
a qualitative illustration that collisional shift sign is state-pair-specific, not a number this
record uses (the transitions, atom and perturbers are all different from Rb 5S-6S self-broadening).

## Absolute measurement: hydrogen 1S-2S (Sec 4.5, pp.821-824)

Read closely because its closing remark is close lineage to this record's own two dominant
apparatus terms. The Hänsch *et al* (1975) / Lee *et al* (1975) two-photon 1S-2S experiment in
atomic hydrogen (Figs 23-24, pp.822-3) is reported with an isotope shift of $670.933\pm0.056$ GHz
(theory: 670.99 GHz) and a 1S Lamb shift of $8.20\pm0.10$ GHz (H) / $8.25\pm0.11$ GHz (D) (p.824,
checked against the rendered page). Then: "The precision of the experiment can still be improved;
the present limitation is due to the linewidth of the laser. Owing to the very long lifetime of
the atom in the metastable 2S state, the limitations which will be found in the future will be the
transit time effect and the light shifts. Both of these limitations are not strictly fundamental
because it is theoretically possible to increase the beam diameter and to reduce the light
intensity." (p.824, checked against the rendered page.) This is offered as historical precedent,
not as a number this record uses: as early as 1977, on a different atom and transition entirely,
transit time and light shift are named as the two systematics expected to dominate a two-photon
Doppler-free measurement once laser linewidth itself is brought under control: the same two
terms this record's own §2.5 (transit-time broadening) and §2.6 (the AC-Stark ramp) build out in
full, on Rb 5S-6S, using a mixture density and a Monte-Carlo transit kernel this 1977 review does
not contain in any form.

## Use in this record

The task's own framing, that this paper would "survey and contextualize the Doppler-free
two-photon technique... and any early treatment of light shifts in that context", is
substantiated on both counts. It surveys and directly cites Biraben's and Bjorkholm/Liao's work
already on this shelf (`biraben1974.md`, `biraben1979.md`, `bjorkholm1976.md`, and the transit-time
citation to `borde1976.md`), and it does give the field's own early light-shift treatment (Sec
2.6, revisited Sec 3.5). But the light-shift treatment is not this record's ancestor in form: it
is a bound-and-avoid argument for a line centre, concluding the shift is usually negligible for
two photons and explicitly not for three, instead of a distributional treatment of the shift as
a signal. What this paper *does* usefully anchor for this record is threefold:

1. The same Baranger (1958a,b,c) impact-approximation citation this record's own collisional-broadening
   derivation rests on, extended here to the specific two-photon argument for a single Lorentzian
   (Sec 4.4.1, 5.5) instead of the bare one-photon citation `docs/methods/02_the_lineshape.md`
   currently carries.
2. Documented 1977-era precedent, on hydrogen 1S-2S, for transit time and the
   light shift being named together as the two systematics expected to dominate a two-photon
   Doppler-free measurement once laser linewidth is controlled, exactly the pairing this record's
   own §2.5/§2.6 build into a full model.
3. The group-theoretic reason (S-S, $L=0\to L=0$, so
   $\Delta F=0$ and $\Delta m_F=0$) that an alkali S-S two-photon transition such as Rb 5S₁/₂-6S₁/₂
   resolves into separate hyperfine-F components at all, worked through explicitly for the analogous
   sodium 3S-5S case on p.807.
