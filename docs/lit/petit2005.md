## What it says, in its own terms

The review sets out the relativistic framework needed to define and compare atomic time scales once clock uncertainties become small enough that special- and general-relativistic effects are no longer negligible. It describes how International Atomic Time (TAI) is built: roughly 250 clocks at about 50 laboratories worldwide are combined monthly into a free-running ensemble scale, EAL, whose rate is then corrected against a small number of primary frequency standards so that TAI's own scale unit matches the SI second's definition. Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), the basis of civil time, is derived from TAI. Comparing clocks separated by thousands of kilometres to construct and disseminate these scales requires synchronization techniques such as GPS-based comparison or two-way satellite time transfer, stated to reach nanosecond-level uncertainty.

The review's central claim is that treating TAI as a realization of a coordinate time in a geocentric reference frame, with time comparisons realized as coordinate synchronizations within that frame, is a relativistic formulation that is adequate for the clock and time-transfer technology available when the paper was written. It then looks ahead to a coming generation of clocks capable of a relative frequency uncertainty of 1e-17 or better, whether laboratory-based or satellite-borne, and to the new time-transfer techniques that would be needed to compare clocks at that level, framing this as the motivation for revisiting the relativistic model in the sections that follow.

## What it is worth here

This is metrology theory for a global network of clocks, not atomic physics: it has no vapour-cell spectroscopy content, no lineshape or Stark-shift physics, and no measurement or apparatus this record could compare against. Its only conceivable point of contact is indirect and speculative -- if the 993 nm Rb 5S-6S two-photon transition were ever proposed as a portable or laboratory frequency reference, comparing it against other clocks across sites would eventually need exactly the relativistic bookkeeping this review describes. Absent that specific use, its relevance to the present programme is marginal.
