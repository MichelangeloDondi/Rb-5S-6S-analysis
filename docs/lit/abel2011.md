# abel2011

VERIFIED against the held preprint (scope in `verify_flags`).

## The method

About 10^6 87Rb atoms from a MOT, released and optically pumped, are probed on the D2 line while a coupling beam near 480 nm drives the 5P3/2 to 46S transition. The EIT feature's position gives the Rydberg level's shift, and with the 46S polarizability, 28.3 MHz/(V/cm)^2, the local field (their Eqs. 1 and 2). Four rod electrodes apply known fields, and the atoms sit 12.5 mm from the window. The introduction states what this reads, verbatim: "The electrometry is carried out in close proximity to an anti-reflection coated fused-silica window and the results are sensitive to the effect of adsorbates on the surface and the polarizability of the dielectric."

## What it finds

With the voltage held on, the shift is not quadratic in the applied field and is fitted with an extra field proportional to the applied one's magnitude and always pointing away from the surface, which the authors attribute to adsorbates, most likely from the dispensers aimed at the window (their Eq. 3 and Fig. 3). The abstract, verbatim: "We show that adsorbates on the dielectric surface produce a significant electric field that responds to an applied field with a time constant of order one second." Pulsing the field for 110 us restores the quadratic Stark map (Fig. 4). Comparing with a two-dimensional solution of Laplace's equation, the window's bulk is represented by a reduced potential with a best-fit factor of 1.6, which the authors note, verbatim: "This value is less than the low frequency permittivity of silica glass, 3.81 [28], suggesting that surface charges play an additional role in determining the field." The quoted sensitivity at n = 46 is about 10 mV/cm.

## Use in this record

For a Rydberg atom near a nanofibre the same two contributions arrive together: a field from adsorbates that follows any applied field with its own relaxation time, and the silica's dielectric response. This paper separates them by timing, a field applied for less than the adsorbates' second-scale response reads the bare Stark map, which is a design rule for any calibration pulse the fibre arm plans. Its geometry is a flat window 12.5 mm away, some four orders of magnitude farther than an atom within a micrometre of a fibre, so it gives the method and the time scale, not a size.

Related on this shelf: `mcguirk2004` and `obrecht2007` (adsorbate dipoles on glass and silica, measured with condensates), `sedlacek2016` (Rb on quartz, measured with Rydberg EIT on cold atoms near the surface).
