# li2024b

**VERIFIED, 2026-08-02**, full text read.

**Not to be confused with [li2024](li2024.md)**, a different Li 2024 on the
same 778 nm line and the same problem. That one is Li Dou and colleagues at
Wuhan, compensating light shift and cell-temperature shift together through a
resonance offset lock. This one is Li Ye and colleagues, cancelling the light
shift alone by interrogating two cells at two powers. Different first author,
different mechanism, same year and same transition, so the citekeys are
li2024 and li2024b.

**The method.** Two Rb vapour cells fed from the same 1556 nm laser doubled to
778 nm, run at two different fixed powers, each generating its own error signal
from lock-in demodulated 420 nm fluorescence. The two are combined in real time
as S = S1 + beta S2, with beta computed from four measured per-system
constants, so that the combined zero crossing sits where the primary system's
light shift vanishes. The authors describe the novelty against the earlier
power-modulation approach as spatial rather than temporal interrogation.

**What it achieves.** The absolute light shift and the light-power sensitivity
each fall by a factor of ten. The residual central offset is about 411 Hz, and
a static test gives 235 Hz with a standard deviation of 55 Hz. The pre-correction
light-shift coefficients are -331 ± 9 and -291 ± 8 Hz/mW.

**Why it is cited here.** The whole construction rests on one equation, the
light shift written as a scalar proportional to interrogation power, with that
power read by a single photodiode per beam. There is no spatial content
anywhere: no beam profile, no intensity distribution, no waist, no Gaussian, no
inhomogeneity, in the model or in the text. What is nulled is the mean shift
and its first-order sensitivity to a change in that scalar power.

That is precisely the pattern [BIG_PICTURE](../BIG_PICTURE.md) §1 describes,
and this is the strongest available evidence for it because the paper is recent,
it is careful, and it succeeds at what it sets out to do. The point is not that
it is wrong. The point is that suppressing a mean leaves the spread over atoms
untouched, and a method built on a single scalar per beam has no representation
of that spread to suppress. Where atoms are held long enough for the spread to
dephase them, that is the term that survives.

**Their own stability figure is not stated in tau form.** The Allan deviation
in their Fig. 5(c) runs from about 1e-12 to 1e-14 over 1 to 100 s but is
characterised only qualitatively, as an order-of-magnitude improvement. The
tau-scaled numbers quoted in the introduction, 3e-13 and 1.8e-13 per root tau,
belong to other groups' systems and should not be attributed to this one.
