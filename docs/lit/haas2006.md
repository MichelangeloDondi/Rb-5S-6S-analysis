# haas2006

VERIFIED. Held (the publisher's typeset PDF, 24 pages, *Phys. Rev. A* **73**, 052501 (2006)). Read in
full, page by page. The prose of Secs. I, II and V, and the framing paragraphs of Secs. III-IV, were read
closely. The eleven large numeric tables of transition matrix elements and Stark/ionization coefficients
(Tables II-VII, XI-XIII) were read for their structure and spot-checked at the rows quoted below, not
verified digit-by-digit against an independent recomputation. The four appendices were read for what they
contain, not derived by hand.

## What the paper actually is

Confirmed against the paper itself, not assumed from the task brief: title, the full 12-author
list, journal, volume, article number and dates were all read directly off page 1, not carried over from
the task's own description. The paper is not a lineshape measurement or a single-trajectory case study. It
is a general theory paper deriving the open-system quantum dynamics: optical Bloch equations for an
effective two-level atom with two loss channels, spontaneous decay and photoionization of the excited
state, for two-photon S-S and S-D transitions in *any* hydrogenlike two-body Coulomb system (hydrogen,
positronium, muonium, antiprotonic helium, ions up to Z=10 are all named, p. 12), plus a large compilation
of the atomic matrix elements and Stark/ionization coefficients those dynamics need. Its own stated
purpose, verbatim (p. 2, 052501-2): "In this paper, we reconsider the derivation of the dynamic
polarizabilities and transition matrix elements for S-S and S-D two-photon transitions in hydrogenlike
systems in a unified treatment, compiling the results in SI units for convenient experimental use." The
abstract itself, verbatim (p. 1, 052501-1, its `(ac)` given in the note's own parentheses, since this
PDF's text layer extracts every parenthesis in the document as a raw glyph code instead of a plain
bracket): "One of the dominant systematic effects that shift resonance lines in high-precision measurements of
two-photon transitions is the dynamic" (ac) "Stark shift." And: "In
order to illustrate the basic mechanisms in the transient regime, we investigate an analytically solvable
model scenario for the population dynamics in the density matrix formalism and describe in detail how to
generalize the corresponding equations of motion for individual experimental use."

## The core original result: an analytic transient lineshape under ionization loss, at constant intensity

Section II sets up the density-matrix (optical Bloch) equations of motion, Eq. (10), with the ac-Stark
shift entering the detuning and photoionization entering as an extra decay rate γ_i on the excited state
(Eqs. 5-9). The worked analytic result (Sec. II C) is the transient excited-state population
ρ'_ee(Δω,t) for *constant* intensity I(t)=I, Eq. (13), given here in the note's own notation since this
PDF's text layer extracts every parenthesis as a raw glyph code: "for constant intensity" I(t)=I ", the
EOMs are a coupled set of first-order differential equations with constant coefficients and hence
are
solvable analytically for all times" (p. 4, 052501-4). This is not a steady-state Lorentzian: for finite
interaction time and non-negligible ionization the solution shows fringes that wash out with increasing
ionization, spectral hole burning, and a maximum excited-state population capped by Eq. (16) instead of
reaching unity even on resonance: "One can hardly overemphasize that the steady state of the density
matrix equation" (10) "is the one where the entire atomic population is in the ionized state" (p. 6,
052501-6, the source's own noun phrase is `the density matrix equation`, not `the system`). The worked
example, Figs. 1-2 (p. 5, 052501-5), is pinned to the parameters of the real hydrogen 1S-2S experiment of
Niering et al. 2000 (this paper's own ref. [16], p. 23, *not* Fischer et al. 2004, a separate ref. [2] in
the same list): laser intensity I=2.3 MW/m², two-photon Rabi frequency Ω=2π×169 Hz (Fig. 1 caption), and
ionization rate γ_i=2π×276 Hz (Fig. 2 caption). For scale, p. 3 (052501-3) states: "the spectral line width
of the laser is on the order of 200 Hz at 121 nm, which is small compared to the typical inverse
interaction time of some kilohertz, while the natural linewidth of the 2S state is only 1.31 Hz."

The paper's own statement of why the width cannot be built from separately-computed pieces (p. 2,
052501-2, Sec. II A, immediately after naming the decisive parameters: the spatial intensity profile,
the second-order Doppler shift, the finite interaction time, and the varying ionization probability):
"The contributions due to the time-of-flight broadening, and the ionization broadening are intertwined
with the quantum dynamics of the excitation process, so that they cannot simply be added in quadrature or
in any other simple algebraic way." This is a non-separability claim of the same *kind* this record's own
AC-Stark chapter makes about the light-shift density and the transit kernel
(`docs/methods/03_the_ac_stark_ramp.md`, its sections Density and composition of the mixture and
Closed-form cost of the mixture), but reached by a different mechanism: Haas et al. couple ionization loss and the Rabi drive
through the same time-dependent density matrix, where this record's own argument is a covariance between
the local shift and the local kernel width across the ensemble of trajectories
(μ₃(X) = -μ₃(u) - 3 Cov(u, V(u))). Both refuse to add symmetric-width and shift/loss terms in quadrature.
Neither argument is a restatement of the other.

## The trajectory / inhomogeneous-beam generalization, and where it is actually carried out

This is the part the task asked to substantiate, and the paper needs a precise reading, not a loose
one. Section II E ("Generalizations of the EOMs") explicitly addresses an atom moving through an
inhomogeneous beam, in the paper's own words (p. 8, 052501-8): "The light intensity in most experiments is
not constant, e.g., the atom under consideration may move through an inhomogeneous laser profile. In this
case, the laser intensity can be described in terms of the trajectory as I(r(t)) ... In general, an
analytic solution including the varying ac Stark effect and Rabi frequency is then either extremely
convoluted or impossible to find. Nonetheless, the EOMs can be readily integrated numerically, for example
using Runge-Kutta routines from Ref. [33], if we include a time-dependent intensity I(t), specific to the
experimental setup, into Eq. (10)." It then states explicitly how an ensemble is built from independent
single-atom trajectories (p. 9, 052501-9): "Finally, in most cases, instead of a single atom one considers
an ensemble of atoms, all of which may experience different intensities, Doppler shifts, and interaction
times along different trajectories. The contribution of each single atom to the line shape can be
calculated exactly like described above, and the line shape produced by the atomic ensemble is then the
sum of all single-atom contributions, given that the single atoms interact independently with the laser."

But the paper is explicit, in three separate places, that the actual numerical/Monte Carlo trajectory
calculation for the real hydrogen 1S-2S line was *not* done in these pages: each time it is attributed to
a separate, unpublished reference. P. 2 (052501-2): "For the hydrogen 1S-2S experiment, this has been
carried out in a Monte Carlo approach [18]." P. 9 (052501-9), immediately after the ensemble-summation
passage quoted above: "Together with the atomic constants presented in the following sections, the set of
equations" (10) "are thus a tool for a wide range of problems in spectroscopy. For a specific implementation
using a Monte Carlo method for the atomic ensemble in the case of the 1S-2S experiment, see [18]." And
p. 18 (052501-18), in the paragraph closing Sec. IV, right before the Conclusions: "In realistic cases, the
effect of an inhomogeneous laser intensity profile and the velocity distributions of an atomic ensemble
can be taken into account with a numerical integration of the equations of motion presented here. Two such
applications can be found in" [18], on the analysis of the hydrogen 1S-2S transition, and in [50],
discussing high-precision spectroscopy in hydrogenlike helium (this scan's text layer renders every
bracketed reference number, like every parenthesis, as a raw glyph code, so the two numbers are given
in the note's own brackets from here on instead of quoted). Reference [18] itself, p. 23 (052501-23):
"N. Kolachevsky, M. Haas, U. D. Jentschura, M. Herrmann, M. Fischer, R. Holzwarth, Th. Udem, C. H. Keitel,
and T. W. Hänsch" (unpublished), nine of this paper's own twelve co-authors (Kolachevsky, Haas,
Jentschura, Herrmann, Fischer, Holzwarth, Udem, Keitel and Hänsch. Only Fendel, Scully and Agarwal are
absent), but a distinct, unpublished work, and not the same reference as Fischer et al. 2004
(`fischer2004`, this paper's own ref. [2], *Phys. Rev. Lett.* **92**, 230802).

So Haas et al. 2006 is the paper that derives the equations of motion a trajectory-resolved treatment
needs (the ac-Stark shift and the ionization-loss channel coupled through the same time-dependent density
matrix), and it states in general terms how to run those equations along an arbitrary atomic trajectory
and sum the result over an ensemble. But the worked, figure-bearing results printed in these 24 pages
(Figs. 1-8) are all computed at *constant* intensity. The trajectory-resolved Monte Carlo lineshape for the
real apparatus is this paper's own citation, not its own content.

## The atomic-constants compilation

Sections III-IV give the two-photon transition matrix element β_ge (Eq. 31, with Table II for 1S/2S⇔nS, Table
III for 1S/2S⇔nD, n up to 20) and the ac-Stark and ionization coefficients β_ac, β_ioni (Eqs. 47-48;
Tables IV-VII) for the same transitions, all tabulated for atomic hydrogen (Z=1, infinite nuclear mass),
with an explicit Z⁻⁴ and reduced-mass scaling law (Eq. 43) to carry the tables to any hydrogenlike
two-body system. Representative row, 1S-2S (Table II, p. 11/052501-11, and Table IV, p. 14/052501-14):
β_ge(1S⇔2S) = 3.68111×10⁻⁵ Hz(W/m²)⁻¹. β_ac(1S) = -2.67827×10⁻⁵ Hz(W/m²)⁻¹, β_ac(2S) = 1.39927×10⁻⁴
Hz(W/m²)⁻¹, β_ioni(2S) = 1.20208×10⁻⁴ Hz(W/m²)⁻¹. Appendix C connects β_ioni to a conventional
photoionization cross section (Eq. C5, p. 20/052501-20). Appendix E cross-checks eighteen of the paper's
own squared matrix elements against Tung et al. 1986 (Table XIII, p. 22/052501-22), agreeing on sixteen.
For the two disagreements (1S-3S and 2S-6S) the paper suggests Tung et al. doubled instead of squared
their 1S-3S value ("likely... inadvertently doubled their correct result... instead of squaring it") and
offers no more than a guess for the 2S-6S case ("might be explained by a twist of digits," p. 22). The
self-consistency of the note's own reading of that table was checked here: 2 x 2.14061 = 4.28122, matching
Tung et al.'s quoted 4.2812 for 1S-3S to the printed precision, and 2.14061² = 4.5822 to the same
precision, matching this paper's own listed value, confirming the doubled-vs-squared account arithmetically.

Relativistic, radiative and field-configuration (finite-beam, non-dipole) corrections to the dynamic
polarizability are given for 1S-2S, 1S-3S and 2S-3S only (Tables VIII-X, pp. 16-17/052501-16/17), as
dimensionless ratios (Eqs. 54-55, 64-66) that combine with a prefactor of order (Zα)² (relativistic) or
α(Zα)²ln[(Zα)⁻²] (radiative) to give the actual relative correction. The paper states these do not exceed
"a relative magnitude of 10⁻⁴" for atomic hydrogen (p. 16/052501-16) and are "found to be small, as
expected, but might become relevant in the future" (p. 18/052501-18), i.e. bounds on how large the
corrections could be, not numbers this paper asks a hydrogen measurement to subtract.

## Use in this record

Confirms, with the caveat above, the task's framing: Haas et al. 2006 is a real precedent for a two-photon
lineshape built by carrying the ac-Stark shift and the atomic trajectory through the *same* dynamical
treatment instead of convolving a fixed shift distribution with a fixed kernel afterward, and it makes
that point explicitly for an atom in an inhomogeneous beam profile (Sec. II E, pp. 8-9, quoted above).
What should not be carried further than the paper supports: the trajectory-resolved *calculation itself*,
for the actual hydrogen 1S-2S line, is this paper's own citation to unpublished work (ref. [18]) and not a
result printed in these 24 pages, and the machinery Haas 2006 built is a full open-system density-matrix
treatment with a photoionization-loss channel, a heavier and physically different machine from this
record's own closed-form geometric ramp mixture (`lineshape.ramp_mixture`,
`docs/methods/03_the_ac_stark_ramp.md`), which carries no ionization channel and needs none at this
bench's intensities and transition (Rb 5S-6S at a vapour cell, not hydrogen 1S-2S excited near a
one-photon continuum with a 121 nm laser). The move the two share is refusing to decouple the shift
distribution from the transit/trajectory kernel by a plain convolution, not the specific machinery each
uses to avoid it.
