VERIFIED, 2026-08-03. Full text read (9 pages).

## The system

Type Ib bulk monocrystalline diamond, NV- density 1-2 ppm, mounted on a cold-finger cryostat. Three approximately 90 fs pulses from an optical parametric amplifier (250 kHz repetition rate) are focused in a box geometry onto the 637 nm zero-phonon line to generate a photon-echo four-wave-mixing (FWM) signal, heterodyne-detected against a local-oscillator pulse routed around the sample.

## The method

Fourier-transforming the echo along two of the three delays gives a one-quantum multi-dimensional coherent spectroscopy (MDCS) spectrum, in which the diagonal direction carries the inhomogeneous strain-broadened linewidth and the orthogonal cross-diagonal direction carries the homogeneous dephasing linewidth. Because NV centers sit in a regime of moderate rather than dominant inhomogeneous broadening, the two directions are fit jointly rather than read off independently.

The homogeneous dephasing rate gamma, tracked from 6 K to 140 K, rises exponentially with temperature and is fit to a single localized phonon-mode occupation model (eq. 1), separating an intrinsic zero-temperature rate gamma0 from a thermally activated amplitude gamma* with characteristic phonon energy Eph. A second measurement holds temperature fixed and low while varying the waiting time T from 1 ps to 2 ns, finding gamma growing linearly with T at two sample locations, an ultrafast spectral-diffusion channel distinct from the thermal one. A third measurement integrates the photon-echo field over the delay tau instead of resolving it in two dimensions, finding two dephasing time constants rather than one, a signature of non-Markovian dephasing at short delay. Diagonal slices of the 2D spectrum develop two resolved lobes as temperature rises, modeled as two fixed-width Gaussian orbital sub-distributions with temperature-dependent centers, attributed to an internal electric field that Stark-splits the excited-state orbital doublet.

## The numbers

- Homogeneous dephasing fit (eq. 1, resonance energy 1945 meV): gamma0 = 37.31 GHz (T2 = 1/gamma0 = 26.8 ps), gamma* = 7890 GHz, Eph = 34.41 meV, matching an independently reported 35 meV vibronic splitting.
- Ultrafast spectral diffusion: gamma grows at 1.98 MHz/ps and 1.59 MHz/ps (two sample spots) as the waiting time T runs from 1 ps to 2 ns.
- Integrated FWM: two dephasing times differing by more than 10 ps at the lowest temperature measured, converging as temperature rises.
- Bimodal inhomogeneous splitting: two Gaussians of fixed widths sigma1 = 2.6 meV (627.8 GHz) and sigma2 = 2.3 meV (555.4 GHz), fitted center separation about 5 meV (40 per cm), read as an internal electric field of 0.29 MV/cm at 50 K rising to 0.43 MV/cm at 140 K via a transverse susceptibility of 1.4 MHz per V/cm.
- ZPL center energy 1946 meV (637 nm). The excitation-pulse bandwidth (about 15 meV half-width) sits below the lowest reported vibronic mode energy (22.6 meV), so the ZPL is addressed without exciting phonon sidebands.

## Comparison with lineshape fitting

A different route to the same underlying problem, recovering a homogeneous dephasing rate from within an inhomogeneously broadened lineshape, is lineshape fitting. There, a perturbation distribution set by known geometry (for example a laser beam's transverse intensity profile, as in `delone1980.md`) is convolved with a homogeneous Lorentzian, and the distribution's cumulants are recovered only within that joint fit, with the distributional form assumed a priori.

MDCS instead places the two contributions on orthogonal axes of a two-dimensional spectrum (diagonal for inhomogeneous, cross-diagonal for homogeneous), a direct consequence of the third-order nonlinear response and the photon-echo phase-rewinding it exploits. No functional form for the inhomogeneous distribution needs to be assumed beforehand, and the separation holds for an arbitrary distribution. The method requires a coherent third-order nonlinear signal and femtosecond pulses. It does not apply where only a continuous-wave linear measurement is available and the perturbation distribution must be inferred rather than known from beam geometry.
